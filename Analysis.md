# Milestone 3 Analysis

## Research Question
If a student in Ontario wants strong early-career employment outcomes, which post-secondary field of study should they enroll in?

## System Archetype: Success to the Successful

The “Success to the Successful” archetype is evident in the relationship between field of study and early-career outcomes. Fields such as health, engineering, and business demonstrate strong employment rates and higher median incomes. As a result, these programs attract more students, receive greater institutional attention, and continue to produce strong outcomes.

In contrast, fields with lower employment rates and lower earnings may experience weaker enrollment growth over time. This creates a reinforcing pattern in which successful programs continue to strengthen while less successful programs become less attractive to students seeking strong early-career outcomes.

The data from Milestone 2 supports this pattern, as stronger employment rates and higher median incomes are concentrated in certain fields of study. This suggests that students respond to visible labour market outcomes, which reinforces the dominance of already successful fields.

## Scenario 1: Status Quo

If current trends continue, students in Ontario will likely continue to favor fields of study associated with strong employment outcomes and higher earnings, such as health, engineering, and business. Over the next 5 to 10 years, this may lead to even greater concentration of enrollment in these programs as students increasingly prioritize job security and income potential when making educational decisions.

As demand for these programs grows, institutions may continue allocating more resources toward them through expanded seats, faculty hiring, and program investment. Meanwhile, fields with weaker employment outcomes may experience lower enrollment and reduced institutional attention. This would reinforce the gap between high-performing and lower-performing fields.

However, the status quo also creates risks. If too many students move into the same high-performing fields, labour market competition may increase, reducing the advantage those fields currently provide. In addition, students may place less value on personal interests or broader educational goals and focus too heavily on short-term employment outcomes. Over time, the system may become more efficient economically, but also more narrow in the educational opportunities students feel comfortable pursuing.

## Scenario 2: Intervention A – Stronger Promotion of High-Outcome Fields

In this scenario, policymakers and post-secondary institutions actively encourage students to pursue fields with stronger early-career outcomes. This could involve improved labour market information, targeted scholarships, enrollment expansion in high-demand programs, or stronger messaging about the employment and income advantages associated with certain fields.

Over the next 5 to 10 years, this would likely increase enrollment in fields such as health, engineering, and business. In the short term, more students may experience stronger employment outcomes because they are choosing programs with clearer labour market demand and stronger income potential. This could improve the overall efficiency of student decision-making and reduce the likelihood of graduates entering fields with weaker short-term outcomes.

At the same time, this approach may create unintended consequences. If the shift becomes too strong, some programs could become oversaturated, reducing employment advantages over time. Students may also feel pressured to choose programs based more on earnings than on skills, motivation, or long-term fit. Although this intervention could improve early-career outcomes overall, it may also create new imbalances if not monitored carefully.

## Scenario 3: Intervention B – Balanced Student Decision Support

In this scenario, policymakers and institutions focus less on pushing students toward specific fields and more on helping them make balanced, informed choices. Instead of heavily promoting only high-income or high-employment programs, the system would provide students with better labour market information, transferable skill development, and more transparent information about both economic outcomes and broader career pathways.

Over the next 5 to 10 years, this may result in a more even distribution of enrollment across fields of study. Students would still be aware of employment and income differences, but they would also be encouraged to consider fit, interests, and long-term adaptability. This could reduce the risk of oversaturation in currently popular programs while still improving decision quality overall.

The tradeoff is that this approach may produce slower short-term gains in employment outcomes compared to a more aggressive shift toward high-performing fields. However, it may create a more stable and sustainable system in the long run by supporting informed choice rather than funneling large numbers of students into the same areas. This scenario balances economic considerations with educational diversity and individual decision-making.

## Leverage Point Analysis

The most promising leverage point in this system is improved access to clear labour market outcome information for students. Many students make education decisions without fully understanding how employment rates, income levels, and long-term prospects differ across fields of study.

Providing clearer and more accessible data on employment outcomes can influence student decision-making without directly restricting choice. This is a high-impact intervention because it helps students align their educational decisions with observed labour market patterns while still preserving flexibility and personal agency.

This leverage point is attractive because it is relatively low-cost compared to major structural reforms. However, there are risks. If students rely too heavily on short-term labour market data, they may undervalue long-term career growth, personal interests, or broader social contributions of certain disciplines. For this reason, information-based interventions should support decision-making rather than oversimplify it.

## Implications for the Decision

The analysis suggests that field of study plays a significant role in shaping early-career employment outcomes. Fields such as health, engineering, and business consistently show stronger employment rates and higher median incomes, making them more attractive choices for students who prioritize short-term labour market success.

At the same time, the analysis shows that educational decisions should not be reduced to employment outcomes alone. If too many students move into the same high-performing fields, labour market competition may increase and the advantages of those fields may weaken over time. In addition, students may make poorer long-term choices if they focus only on salary or employment rates without considering personal fit and career sustainability.

The most promising approach appears to be one that gives students clearer labour market information while still supporting a range of educational pathways. This helps students make better-informed choices without forcing them into a narrow set of programs. These findings will support the final recommendation in Milestone 4.
